The rule that catches people out

Anything that arrives in a shipping container costs roughly the same everywhere. Anything done by a person is where the money actually moves.

Every item is split into five cost drivers: traded goods, local labour, premises, local retail and energy. Each city prices those drivers differently, and items with their own economics carry an adjustment on top: alcohol duty, fuel subsidy, vehicle tax, medical pricing, and so on. That is why two things in the same category move by different amounts. Calibration sources are credited on the Mission tab. These figures show the shape of a difference, not a receipt.

Why GeoEarn exists

Moving country should not be a guess.

More people than ever earn their living from a laptop and can, in theory, do it from anywhere. In practice almost nobody can answer the one question that decides whether the move works: if I go there, what does my work need to be worth?

GeoEarn answers it in about forty seconds, then shows the working.

The answer is scattered

Rent sits on a listings site, tax on a government page written for accountants, visa rules in a PDF, and the honest version of daily life in a forum thread from 2019. Nobody assembles them, so most people move on vibes and a screenshot of an apartment.

One rate, quoted everywhere

Freelancers price their work once and carry that number across borders. Some quietly lose money after a move. Others leave a large amount of freedom on the table because they never checked how far the same rate stretches somewhere else.

Averages hide the truth

A single cost of living percentage tells you nothing useful. Rent might halve while your laptop, your phone and your flights cost the same or more. What changes and what does not is the whole story.

What we believe

Location changes what money buys.
It does not change what your work is worth.

The gap between those two facts is where the opportunity sits. GeoEarn measures that gap precisely, in the only unit a freelancer actually negotiates with: an hourly rate.

How it works

Three steps, in this order, because each one depends on the last.

01

Describe the life you have

Your income, your rate, your city, and how comfortably you live on it. From that we work out what you actually spend and what you actually keep, which is the part every generic calculator skips.

02

Point at somewhere new

We rebuild that same life in the destination: rent at your standard, groceries, transport, healthcare, and the local tax bite. Nothing is downgraded to make the numbers look good.

03

Read the gap

Out comes one number: the hourly rate that keeps your lifestyle and your savings exactly where they are. The distance between that and what you charge today is your room to move.

Who it is built for

Three people with the same question and no good way to answer it.

The freelancer with one client and an idea

Wants to know whether leaving an expensive city means a pay cut, or simply a better life at the same rate.

The graduate choosing a first base

Has a remote offer and no instinct yet for what a salary is worth once rent, tax and a visa are taken out of it.

The family weighing a real move

Cares less about the arbitrage and more about school fees, healthcare, and what a weekly shop costs on the ground.

What we will not pretend

A tool about money is only useful if it is honest about its own limits.

The dataset is modelled, not live. Every figure is an informed estimate built from published indices. Confirm rent and tax before you sign anything.

This is not tax advice. Effective rates stand in for a real filing position, which depends on residency, treaties and how you invoice.

Nothing is collected. There is no server, no analytics and no account database. Your scenarios never leave the browser you typed them into.

The advisor is not a chatbot. It answers a fixed set of questions from the dataset, so it can never invent a number to sound helpful.

Where the numbers come from

GeoEarn does not redistribute anybody else's dataset. The city indices and the item level price model are ours, and they are calibrated against these public references.

Calibration sources
World Bank International Comparison Program

The 2021 cycle publishes price level indices for 176 economies across 45 separate expenditure headings. That structure is the reason GeoEarn prices each category on its own drivers rather than one blended index.

Numbeo cost of living indices

Crowd sourced city level indices built on a New York equals 100 baseline. GeoEarn follows the same convention for its rent, everyday and health indices so the two are readable side by side.

Purchasing power parity conversion factors

Maintained by the World Bank with the OECD and Eurostat PPP programmes. Used to sanity check that the ratios between regions stay in a believable range rather than drifting on their own.

Speed rankings and government visa pages

Median fixed line speeds follow publicly reported national broadband rankings. Every visa route, and the income floor attached to it, comes from the issuing country's own published guidance.

How a single price is built

Each of the 79 tracked items is split into five cost drivers: traded goods, local labour, premises, local retail and energy. A television is almost entirely traded goods, so it barely moves between cities. A haircut is almost entirely labour, so it moves enormously. On top of that, items with their own local economics carry a specific adjustment: alcohol duty, fuel subsidy, vehicle tax, medical pricing, mobile and broadband competition, and produce that grows locally in one place and is flown into another. Two things in the same category therefore give different answers, which is the whole point.

The cost of a life is knowable. It just was not written down in one place until now.
